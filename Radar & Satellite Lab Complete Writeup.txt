EXPERIMENT 1: RADAR RANGE EQUATION SIMULATION
================================================================================

1. AIM:
   To simulate the Radar Range Equation in MATLAB and analyze the effect of 
   Peak Transmitted Power (Pt), Antenna Gain (G), Operating Frequency (f), and 
   Target Radar Cross Section (RCS) on the Maximum Detection Range (Rmax).

2. APPARATUS / SOFTWARE REQUIRED:
   - PC / Laptop with Windows/Linux OS
   - MATLAB (R2020a or later)

3. BLOCK DIAGRAM (BASIC MONOSTATIC RADAR SYSTEM):
   +-------------------+      +-------------------+      +-----------------+
   |    Transmitter    | ---> |     Duplexer      | ---> |   Directional   |
   |  (Peak Power Pt)  |      | (Tx / Rx Switch)  |      |  Antenna (Gain G)|
   +-------------------+      +-------------------+      +-----------------+
                                       |                          |
                                       v                          | (RF Wave)
                              +-------------------+               v
                              |     Receiver      |      +-----------------+
                              | (Sensitivity Pmin)| <--- |  Target (RCS)   |
                              +-------------------+      +-----------------+
                                       |
                                       v
                              +-------------------+
                              |  Radar Display /  |
                              |  Range Processor  |
                              +-------------------+

4. THEORY & FORMULAS:
   The Radar Range Equation relates the maximum detection distance (Rmax) to 
   the physical parameters of the radar transmitter, antenna, propagation 
   medium, target, and receiver:

   - Wavelength:
     lambda = c / f

   - Maximum Unambiguous Detection Range:
     Rmax = [ (Pt * G^2 * lambda^2 * sigma) / ((4 * pi)^3 * Pmin) ]^(1/4)

   - Received Echo Power at Distance R:
     Pr = (Pt * G^2 * lambda^2 * sigma) / ((4 * pi)^3 * R^4)

   Where:
     Pt     = Peak Transmitted Power (Watts)
     G      = Antenna Gain (dimensionless linear ratio)
     lambda = Operating Wavelength (meters)
     sigma  = Target Radar Cross Section, RCS (m^2)
     Pmin   = Minimum Detectable Signal Power at Receiver (Watts)
     c      = Speed of Light = 3 x 10^8 m/s

   Key Observation:
   Because Rmax is proportional to the 4th root of Pt and sigma, increasing 
   transmitted power by 16 times only doubles the maximum detection range.

5. PROCEDURE:
   Step 1: Open MATLAB and clear the workspace using clc, clear, close all.
   Step 2: Initialize baseline radar parameters: Pt = 10 kW, G = 1000, 
           lambda = 0.03 m (f = 10 GHz), sigma = 1 m^2, Pmin = 1e-13 W.
   Step 3: Define an anonymous function Rm for the Radar Range Equation.
   Step 4: Compute and display the baseline Rmax in kilometers (14.59 km).
   Step 5: Vary one parameter at a time (Pt, G, f, sigma) while keeping others 
           constant, and plot the results in a 2x2 subplot window.

6. MATLAB CODE:
   clc; clear; close all;
   Pt = 10e3; G = 1000; lam = 0.03; s = 1; Pmin = 1e-13;
   Rm = @(Pt,G,l,s) (Pt.*G.^2.*l.^2.*s./((4*pi)^3*Pmin)).^(1/4);
   fprintf('Base Rmax = %.2f km\n', Rm(Pt,G,lam,s)/1e3);

   subplot(2,2,1); plot(1:100, Rm((1:100)*1e3,G,lam,s)/1e3); xlabel('Pt (kW)'); ylabel('Rmax (km)'); title('Pt vs Rmax'); grid on;
   subplot(2,2,2); plot(10:1000, Rm(Pt,10:1000,lam,s)/1e3); xlabel('Gain'); ylabel('Rmax (km)'); title('Gain vs Rmax'); grid on;
   subplot(2,2,3); plot(1:20, Rm(Pt,G,3e8./((1:20)*1e9),s)/1e3); xlabel('Freq (GHz)'); ylabel('Rmax (km)'); title('Freq vs Rmax'); grid on;
   subplot(2,2,4); plot(0.1:0.1:10, Rm(Pt,G,lam,0.1:0.1:10)/1e3); xlabel('RCS (m^2)'); ylabel('Rmax (km)'); title('RCS vs Rmax'); grid on;

7. EXPECTED OUTPUT & GRAPH ANALYSIS:
   - Command Window: Base Rmax = 14.59 km
   - Subplot 1 (Pt vs Rmax): Concave 4th-root curve showing diminishing range 
     returns as transmitted power increases from 1 kW to 100 kW.
   - Subplot 2 (Gain vs Rmax): Square-root growth curve (Rmax proportional to 
     sqrt(G)), showing stronger sensitivity than power.
   - Subplot 3 (Freq vs Rmax): Decaying curve; higher frequency shrinks 
     wavelength (lambda), reducing antenna capture area and detection range.
   - Subplot 4 (RCS vs Rmax): Concave 4th-root growth curve; larger targets 
     reflect more energy and are detected farther away.

8. CONCLUSION:
   The Radar Range Equation was successfully simulated in MATLAB. It is 
   concluded that Rmax is proportional to (Pt)^(1/4), G^(1/2), lambda^(1/2), 
   and (sigma)^(1/4). Enhancing antenna gain and receiver sensitivity is far 
   more efficient for extending range than solely increasing transmit power.

EXPERIMENT 2: RADAR TARGET DETECTION IN NOISE (ROC CURVES)
================================================================================

1. AIM:
   To simulate radar target detection in Additive White Gaussian Noise (AWGN) 
   using an Energy Detector and plot Probability of Detection (Pd) versus SNR 
   and Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves (Pd vs Pfa).

2. APPARATUS / SOFTWARE REQUIRED:
   - PC / Laptop with MATLAB installed

3. BLOCK DIAGRAM (RADAR THRESHOLD / ENERGY DETECTOR):
   +-------------------+      +-------------------+      +-------------------+
   |  Received Signal  | ---> |  Square-Law /     | ---> |    Integrator     |
   |  y[n] = s[n]+w[n] |      |  Energy Detector  |      |  E = Sum(|y|^2)   |
   +-------------------+      +-------------------+      +-------------------+
                                                                   |
                                                                   v
   +-------------------+      +-------------------+      +-------------------+
   |  Decision Output  | <--- |    Comparator     | <--- |  Threshold Level  |
   |  H1 (Target) / H0 |      |   Is E > gamma?   |      |  (Set by Pfa)     |
   +-------------------+      +-------------------+      +-------------------+

4. THEORY & FORMULAS:
   In real radar receivers, weak target echoes are corrupted by thermal noise 
   (AWGN). Detection is formulated as a binary hypothesis test:
     - H0 (Null Hypothesis)       : y[n] = w[n]        (Noise Only)
     - H1 (Alternative Hypothesis): y[n] = s[n] + w[n] (Target + Noise)

   - Energy Detector Test Statistic:
     E = sum( |y[n]|^2 ) for n = 1 to N

   - Performance Metrics:
     1) Probability of False Alarm (Pfa): Probability that noise alone exceeds 
        the threshold gamma under H0:  Pfa = P(E > gamma | H0)
     2) Probability of Detection (Pd): Probability that target + noise exceeds 
        the threshold gamma under H1:  Pd = P(E > gamma | H1)

   - Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC):
     A plot of Pd versus Pfa for a fixed SNR. Lowering threshold gamma 
     increases Pd (better sensitivity) at the cost of higher Pfa (more false 
     alarms).

5. PROCEDURE:
   Step 1: Generate a 1000-sample frame with a rectangular target pulse placed 
           between samples 401 and 500.
   Step 2: Sweep SNR from -10 dB to +10 dB over M = 2000 Monte Carlo trials.
   Step 3: Compute noise-only (H0) energies and set threshold gamma at the 
           99th percentile to maintain Pfa = 0.01.
   Step 4: Compute signal+noise (H1) energies and calculate Pd at each SNR.
   Step 5: Fix SNR = -8 dB, sweep Pfa from 10^-3 to 1, and plot the ROC curve.

6. MATLAB CODE:
   clc; clear; close all;
   N = 1000; p = zeros(1,N); p(401:500) = 1;
   snr = -10:2:10; M = 2000; Pfa = 1e-2;

   for k = 1:numel(snr)
       sg = sqrt(mean(p.^2) / 10^(snr(k)/10));
       e0 = sort(sum((sg*randn(M,N)).^2, 2));
       e1 = sum((p + sg*randn(M,N)).^2, 2);
       Pd(k) = mean(e1 > e0(ceil((1-Pfa)*M)));
   end
   figure; plot(snr, Pd, 'o-'); xlabel('SNR (dB)'); ylabel('Pd'); title('Pd vs SNR (Pfa = 0.01)'); grid on;

   sg = sqrt(0.1 / 10^(-0.8));
   e0 = sort(sum((sg*randn(M,N)).^2, 2));
   e1 = sum((p + sg*randn(M,N)).^2, 2);
   Pf = logspace(-3, 0, 30);
   for i = 1:30
       Pd2(i) = mean(e1 > e0(max(1, floor((1-Pf(i))*M))));
   end
   figure; semilogx(Pf, Pd2, 'o-'); xlabel('Pfa'); ylabel('Pd'); title('ROC Curve (at -8 dB SNR)'); grid on;

7. EXPECTED OUTPUT & GRAPH ANALYSIS:
   - Figure 1 (Pd vs SNR): S-shaped sigmoidal curve. Pd is near 0 at -10 dB, 
     rises sharply between -4 dB and 0 dB, and saturates at Pd = 1.0 above 0 dB.
   - Figure 2 (ROC Curve): Logarithmic curve showing Pd rising smoothly from 
     near 0 to 1 as Pfa increases from 10^-3 to 10^0 at -8 dB SNR.

8. CONCLUSION:
   Radar target detection in AWGN was simulated using Monte Carlo trials. The 
   results confirm that higher SNR significantly improves detection reliability 
   and demonstrate the fundamental Neyman-Pearson trade-off between maximizing 
   Pd and minimizing Pfa.

EXPERIMENT 3: DOPPLER FREQUENCY SHIFT SIMULATION
================================================================================

1. AIM:
   To simulate the Doppler frequency shift caused by a moving radar target, 
   extract the frequency shift using Fast Fourier Transform (FFT), and study its 
   dependence on target radial velocity and radar carrier frequency.

2. APPARATUS / SOFTWARE REQUIRED:
   - PC / Laptop with MATLAB installed

3. BLOCK DIAGRAM (CW DOPPLER RADAR SYSTEM):
   +-------------------+      +-------------------+      +-----------------+
   |  RF Oscillator    | ---> | Power Amplifier / | ---> |   Tx Antenna    |
   |  (Frequency fc)   |      |     Splitter      |      |                 |
   +-------------------+      +-------------------+      +-----------------+
                                       |                          |
                                       | (Reference fc)           v (fc +/- fd)
                                       v                 +-----------------+
   +-------------------+      +-------------------+      |  Moving Target  |
   |  FFT Spectrum /   | <--- |  Mixer & Low-Pass | <--- |  (Velocity v)   |
   |  Speed Display    |      |   Filter (|fd|)   |      +-----------------+
   +-------------------+      +-------------------+

4. THEORY & FORMULAS:
   When a target moves with radial velocity (v) relative to a stationary radar 
   operating at carrier frequency (fc), the reflected echo undergoes a Doppler 
   frequency shift (fd):

   - Two-Way Doppler Frequency Shift:
     fd = (2 * v * fc) / c = (2 * v) / lambda

   - Received Signal Frequency:
     f_rx = fc + fd   (Target moving toward radar)
     f_rx = fc - fd   (Target moving away from radar)

   Where:
     v  = Radial velocity of target (m/s)
     fc = Radar carrier frequency (Hz)
     c  = Speed of light (3 x 10^8 m/s)

   Taking the FFT of the received baseband signal produces spectral peaks at 
   +fd and -fd, allowing direct calculation of target speed: v = (fd * c)/(2*fc).

5. PROCEDURE:
   Step 1: Define c = 3e8 m/s, sampling frequency Fs = 100 kHz, and time axis.
   Step 2: Set carrier frequency fc = 10 GHz and vary velocity v = 10 to 50 m/s.
   Step 3: Compute fd for each velocity and display the [v, fd] table.
   Step 4: Generate cosine Doppler signals and plot their double-sided FFT 
           magnitude spectra in 5 subplots.
   Step 5: Fix v = 30 m/s, sweep carrier frequency fc from 2 to 20 GHz, and 
           plot fd versus fc.

6. MATLAB CODE:
   clc; clear; close all;
   c = 3e8; Fs = 1e5; t = 0:1/Fs:0.1-1/Fs;
   v = 10:10:50; fc = 10e9; fd = 2*v*fc/c;
   disp([v; fd]');

   N = numel(t); f = (-N/2:N/2-1)*Fs/N;
   figure;
   for k = 1:5
       subplot(5,1,k);
       plot(f, abs(fftshift(fft(cos(2*pi*fd(k)*t)))/N));
       xlim([-5e3 5e3]); xlabel('Freq (Hz)'); ylabel('Mag'); title(['fd = ' num2str(round(fd(k),1)) ' Hz']); grid on;
   end

   fc2 = [2 5 10 15 20]*1e9; fd2 = 2*30*fc2/c;
   figure; plot(fc2/1e9, fd2, '-o'); xlabel('Carrier Frequency (GHz)'); ylabel('Doppler Shift (Hz)'); title('fd vs fc (at v = 30 m/s)'); grid on;

7. EXPECTED OUTPUT & GRAPH ANALYSIS:
   - Command Window Table:
     v = 10 m/s -> fd = 666.7 Hz; v = 30 m/s -> fd = 2000 Hz; v = 50 m/s -> 3333.3 Hz.
   - Figure 1 (FFT Spectra): Each subplot exhibits two sharp symmetric peaks at 
     +fd and -fd (because cos(2*pi*fd*t) contains positive and negative 
     frequency components) that shift outward as target velocity increases.
   - Figure 2 (fd vs fc): A straight line passing through the origin, proving 
     linear proportionality between Doppler shift and carrier frequency.

8. CONCLUSION:
   Doppler frequency shift was simulated and verified using spectral FFT. The 
   Doppler shift varies linearly with both target radial velocity and carrier 
   frequency, enabling precise velocity measurement in CW and pulsed radars.

EXPERIMENT 4: MTI RADAR USING DELAY LINE CANCELLER
================================================================================

1. AIM:
   To simulate a Moving Target Indicator (MTI) radar using a Single Delay Line 
   Canceller in MATLAB and demonstrate the suppression of stationary ground 
   clutter while retaining moving target echoes.

2. APPARATUS / SOFTWARE REQUIRED:
   - PC / Laptop with MATLAB installed

3. BLOCK DIAGRAM (SINGLE DELAY LINE CANCELLER):
   Received Signal x[n] --------+----------------------> (+) ---> Output y[n]
                                |                         ^
                                v                         | (-)
                       +-----------------+                |
                       |  1-Pulse Delay  | ---------------+
                       |  (z^-1 = PRI)   |   x[n-1]
                       +-----------------+

4. THEORY & FORMULAS:
   Ground-based radars receive strong unwanted echoes (clutter) from stationary 
   objects such as mountains, buildings, and trees. Because stationary clutter 
   has zero Doppler shift (fd = 0), its amplitude and phase remain constant 
   from pulse to pulse.

   - Single Delay Line Canceller Difference Equation:
     y[n] = x[n] - x[n-1]

   - Z-Domain Transfer Function:
     H(z) = 1 - z^(-1)

   - Frequency Response Magnitude:
     |H(f)| = 2 * |sin(pi * f * PRI)|

   - Blind Speeds (Nulls where |H(f)| = 0):
     v_blind = (n * lambda * PRF) / 2   (n = 1, 2, 3...)

   When consecutive pulses are subtracted, stationary clutter cancels out 
   completely (y[n] = 0), whereas moving targets undergo a pulse-to-pulse 
   phase shift and pass through to the output.

5. PROCEDURE:
   Step 1: Generate N = 1000 pulse samples.
   Step 2: Create a stationary DC clutter signal (cl = 1 + small random noise).
   Step 3: Create a moving target Doppler sine wave (tg = sin(2*pi*0.08*t)).
   Step 4: Combine clutter, target, and receiver noise into received signal rx.
   Step 5: Apply the single delay line canceller using diff(rx) and plot all 
           four waveforms in a 4x1 subplot layout.

6. MATLAB CODE:
   clc; clear; close all;
   N = 1000; t = 0:N-1;
   cl = 1 + 0.1*randn(1,N);
   tg = sin(2*pi*0.08*t);
   rx = cl + tg + 0.1*randn(1,N);
   mti = diff(rx);

   subplot(4,1,1); plot(cl); ylabel('Amp'); title('1. Stationary Clutter (DC)'); grid on;
   subplot(4,1,2); plot(tg); ylabel('Amp'); title('2. Moving Target Signal'); grid on;
   subplot(4,1,3); plot(rx); ylabel('Amp'); title('3. Received Signal (Clutter + Target + Noise)'); grid on;
   subplot(4,1,4); plot(mti); xlabel('Pulse Number'); ylabel('Amp'); title('4. MTI Output (Clutter Cancelled)'); grid on;

7. EXPECTED OUTPUT & GRAPH ANALYSIS:
   - Subplot 1 (Clutter): High constant DC baseline centered around +1.0.
   - Subplot 2 (Moving Target): Clean sinusoidal oscillation centered at 0.
   - Subplot 3 (Received Signal): Target sine wave shifted upward onto the +1.0 
     clutter baseline.
   - Subplot 4 (MTI Output): The +1.0 DC clutter offset is completely removed, 
     restoring the oscillating moving target waveform around zero.

8. CONCLUSION:
   The Single Delay Line Canceller was successfully simulated. It acts as a 
   comb/high-pass filter with nulls at zero Doppler frequency, effectively 
   eliminating stationary clutter while preserving moving target detections.

EXPERIMENT 5: PULSE DOPPLER RADAR TARGET DETECTION
================================================================================

1. AIM:
   To simulate a coherent Pulse Doppler Radar using Linear Frequency Modulated 
   (LFM) chirp pulses, Matched Filtering, and 2D Slow-Time Doppler FFT to 
   simultaneously detect range and velocity of multiple targets.

2. APPARATUS / SOFTWARE REQUIRED:
   - PC / Laptop with MATLAB installed

3. BLOCK DIAGRAM (PULSE DOPPLER SIGNAL PROCESSING CHAIN):
   +---------------+    +----------------+    +---------------+    +-----------+
   |  LFM Chirp    | -> | Received 2D    | -> | Matched Filter| -> | Hamming   |
   |  Generator    |    | Data Matrix rx |    | (Fast-Time)   |    | Window    |
   +---------------+    +----------------+    +---------------+    +-----------+
                                                                          |
                                                                          v
                                              +---------------+    +-----------+
                                              | 2D Range-     | <- | Slow-Time |
                                              | Doppler Map   |    | FFT (2D)  |
                                              +---------------+    +-----------+

4. THEORY & FORMULAS:
   Pulse Doppler Radar transmits a coherent burst of Np pulses and arranges 
   received samples into a 2D matrix (Fast-Time Range Bins x Slow-Time Pulses):

   - LFM Chirp Waveform:
     s(t) = exp( j * pi * (BW / pw) * (t - pw/2)^2 )

   - Fast-Time Processing (Range Compression):
     Each pulse is correlated with the time-reversed conjugate of the 
     transmitted chirp via a Matched Filter: h(t) = s*(pw - t), yielding sharp 
     range peaks with resolution Delta_R = c / (2 * BW).

   - Slow-Time Processing (Velocity Extraction):
     Taking an FFT across the Np pulses extracts pulse-to-pulse Doppler phase 
     shifts, giving radial velocity: v = (fd * lambda) / 2.

   - Unambiguous Limits:
     Rmax = c / (2 * PRF) = 30 km,    Vmax = (lambda * PRF) / 4 = +/- 37.5 m/s

5. PROCEDURE:
   Step 1: Define radar parameters: fc = 10 GHz, PRF = 5 kHz, Fs = 20 MHz, 
           pw = 10 us, BW = 5 MHz, Np = 64 pulses.
   Step 2: Define three targets: (5 km, +20 m/s), (12 km, -15 m/s), and 
           (20 km, +8 m/s).
   Step 3: Synthesize the LFM chirp pulse and populate the 2D received matrix 
           with range delays, Doppler phase shifts, and AWGN noise (10 dB SNR).
   Step 4: Apply Matched Filtering along range bins and Doppler FFT with a 
           Hamming window across the 64 pulses.
   Step 5: Plot the normalized 2D Range-Doppler Map in decibels (dB).

6. MATLAB CODE:
   clc; clear; close all;
   c = 3e8; fc = 10e9; lam = c/fc; PRF = 5e3; Fs = 20e6; pw = 10e-6; BW = 5e6; Np = 64;
   R = [5000 12000 20000]; V = [20 -15 8];
   Ns = Fs/PRF; Npw = Fs*pw;

   t = (0:Npw-1)'/Fs;
   tx = exp(1j*pi*BW/pw*(t-pw/2).^2);
   rx = zeros(Ns, Np);
   for n = 1:3
       d = round(2*R(n)/c*Fs);
       rx(d+1:d+Npw,:) = rx(d+1:d+Npw,:) + tx*exp(1j*2*pi*2*V(n)/lam*(0:Np-1)/PRF);
   end
   rx = awgn(rx, 10, 'measured');

   y = filter(conj(flipud(tx)), 1, rx);
   D = fftshift(fft(y.*hamming(Np)', 128, 2), 2);
   r = (0:Ns-1)*c/(2*Fs);
   v = (-64:63)*PRF/128*lam/2;

   imagesc(v, r/1e3, 20*log10(abs(D)/max(abs(D(:))))); axis xy; colorbar; clim([-50 0]);
   xlabel('Velocity (m/s)'); ylabel('Range (km)'); title('2D Range-Doppler Map (dB)');

7. EXPECTED OUTPUT & GRAPH ANALYSIS:
   - 2D Range-Doppler Heatmap: Displays a suppressed noise background (-50 dB) 
     with three distinct high-intensity peaks corresponding to all three moving 
     targets at their respective (Velocity, Range) coordinates:
     Target 1 (+20 m/s, ~6.5 km), Target 2 (-15 m/s, ~13.5 km), Target 3 
     (+8 m/s, ~21.5 km), including the matched filter group delay offset.

8. CONCLUSION:
   Pulse Doppler radar target detection was simulated in MATLAB. Combining LFM 
   Matched Filtering (fast-time) and Doppler FFT (slow-time) accurately 
   resolves both range and radial velocity of multiple targets in noise.

EXPERIMENT 6: RADAR DISPLAY SIMULATION (A-SCAN, B-SCAN, PPI)
================================================================================

1. AIM:
   To simulate standard radar display formats—A-Scan, B-Scan, and Plan 
   Position Indicator (PPI)—in MATLAB with a 360-degree rotating antenna sweep.

2. APPARATUS / SOFTWARE REQUIRED:
   - PC / Laptop with MATLAB installed

3. BLOCK DIAGRAM (RADAR DISPLAY COORDINATE PROCESSING):
   +-------------------+      +-------------------+      +-------------------+
   |   Rotating Radar  | ---> |  Echo Detection   | ---> |  A-Scan Display   |
   |  Antenna (0-360)  |      |  (Range R, Az)    |      |  (Amp vs Range)   |
   +-------------------+      +-------------------+      +-------------------+
                                       |
                        +--------------+--------------+
                        v                             v
               +-----------------+           +-----------------+
               | B-Scan Display  |           | Polar-to-Cart.  |
               | (Range vs Az)   |           | X = R*sin(Az)   |
               +-----------------+           | Y = R*cos(Az)   |
                                             +-----------------+
                                                      |
                                                      v
                                             +-----------------+
                                             |   PPI Display   |
                                             | (360 Polar Map) |
                                             +-----------------+

4. THEORY & FORMULAS:
   Radar receivers extract target Range (R) and Azimuth angle (Az) in polar 
   coordinates and render them on three primary operator screens:
   1) A-Scan: 1D plot of Echo Amplitude (Y-axis) vs Range (X-axis).
   2) B-Scan: 2D rectangular plot of Range (Y-axis) vs Azimuth Angle (X-axis).
   3) PPI (Plan Position Indicator): 2D circular map centered on the radar 
      (0,0) with a rotating sweep line. Because radar North is 0 degrees 
      clockwise, polar-to-Cartesian conversion uses:
      X = R * sin(Azimuth)
      Y = R * cos(Azimuth)

   Phosphor persistence is simulated using exponential decay: B_new = 0.99*B_old.

5. PROCEDURE:
   Step 1: Define 4 targets at ranges R = [25, 50, 75, 60] km and angles 
           Az = [40, 120, 220, 300] degrees.
   Step 2: Run a loop from ang = 1 to 360 degrees to simulate antenna rotation.
   Step 3: Detect when the antenna beam (+/- 4 degrees) illuminates a target.
   Step 4: Simultaneously update and animate the A-Scan, B-Scan, and circular 
           PPI display with concentric range rings using drawnow.

6. MATLAB CODE:
   clc; clear; close all;
   r = 0:100; th = linspace(0,2*pi,200);
   R = [25 50 75 60]; Az = [40 120 220 300];
   B = zeros(101, 360);

   for ang = 1:360
       hit = abs(mod(Az - ang + 180, 360) - 180) <= 4;
       a = 0.02*rand(size(r)); B = 0.99*B;
       for k = find(hit)
           a = a + exp(-((r - R(k))/2).^2);
           B(round(R(k))+1, Az(k)+1) = 1;
       end
       subplot(1,3,1); plot(r, a); ylim([0 1.2]); xlabel('Range (km)'); ylabel('Amp'); title('A-Scan');
       subplot(1,3,2); imagesc(0:359, r, B); axis xy; xlabel('Azimuth (deg)'); ylabel('Range (km)'); title('B-Scan');
       subplot(1,3,3); plot(R.*sind(Az), R.*cosd(Az), 'ro', [0 100*sind(ang)], [0 100*cosd(ang)], 'k', sin(th)'*(25:25:100), cos(th)'*(25:25:100), ':');
       axis equal; axis([-100 100 -100 100]); xlabel('East-West (km)'); ylabel('North-South (km)'); title('PPI Display');
       drawnow;
   end

7. EXPECTED OUTPUT & GRAPH ANALYSIS:
   - Subplot 1 (A-Scan): Shows receiver noise floor with sharp Gaussian spikes 
     appearing whenever the antenna beam sweeps across a target's range.
   - Subplot 2 (B-Scan): Rectangular grid displaying bright target blips at 
     (40, 25), (120, 50), (220, 75), and (300, 60) with gradual fading trails.
   - Subplot 3 (PPI Display): Circular radar map with 4 range rings (25 to 
     100 km), a rotating black sweep line, and 4 red target markers plotted at 
     their true bird's-eye spatial positions.

8. CONCLUSION:
   A-Scan, B-Scan, and PPI radar displays were simulated in MATLAB. The PPI 
   display provides the clearest bird's-eye spatial awareness by converting 
   polar range-azimuth measurements into true 2D Cartesian coordinates.

EXPERIMENT 7: SATELLITE LINK BUDGET ANALYSIS
================================================================================

1. AIM:
   To perform a complete Satellite Link Budget analysis in MATLAB by calculating 
   Antenna Gains (Gt, Gr), EIRP, Free Space Path Loss (FSPL), Received Power 
   (Pr), Thermal Noise (N), Carrier-to-Noise Ratio (C/N), and Link Margin.

2. APPARATUS / SOFTWARE REQUIRED:
   - PC / Laptop with MATLAB installed

3. BLOCK DIAGRAM (SATELLITE DOWNLINK POWER BUDGET):
   +-------------------+      +-------------------+      +-------------------+
   | Satellite Tx      | ---> |  Tx Dish Antenna  | ---> |  Free Space Path  |
   | (Pt = 100 W, Lt)  |      |  (Dt = 2.4 m, Gt) |      |  (FSPL + Rain)    |
   +-------------------+      +-------------------+      +-------------------+
                                                                   |
                                                                   v
   +-------------------+      +-------------------+      +-------------------+
   | Demodulator       | <--- |  Receiver LNA     | <--- |  Rx Dish Antenna  |
   | (C/N & Margin)    |      |  (T=500K, B=36MHz)|      |  (Dr = 1.8 m, Gr) |
   +-------------------+      +-------------------+      +-------------------+

4. THEORY & FORMULAS:
   A Satellite Link Budget accounts for all gains and losses (in decibels) from 
   the transmitter to the receiver:

   - Parabolic Antenna Gain:
     G (dBi) = 10 * log10( eta * (pi * D / lambda)^2 )

   - Equivalent Isotropically Radiated Power (EIRP):
     EIRP (dBW) = Pt (dBW) + Gt (dBi) - Lt (dB)

   - Free Space Path Loss (FSPL):
     FSPL (dB) = 20 * log10(4 * pi * R / lambda)

   - Received Signal Power (Pr):
     Pr (dBW) = EIRP - FSPL + Gr - L_other - L_rain

   - Receiver Thermal Noise Power (N):
     N (dBW) = 10 * log10(k * T * B)

   - Carrier-to-Noise Ratio (C/N) & Link Margin:
     C/N (dB)    = Pr - N
     Margin (dB) = (C/N)_achieved - (C/N)_required

   If Margin >= 0 dB, the communication link is FEASIBLE.

5. PROCEDURE:
   Step 1: Set f = 12 GHz, R = 38,000 km, Pt = 100 W (20 dBW), Dt = 2.4 m 
           (eta = 65%), Dr = 1.8 m (eta = 60%), Lt = 2 dB, L_other = 1.5 dB, 
           L_rain = 2 dB, T = 500 K, B = 36 MHz, and Required C/N = 10 dB.
   Step 2: Calculate wavelength lambda, Gt, Gr, EIRP, FSPL, Pr, N, C/N, and 
           Link Margin, and print all results in the Command Window.
   Step 3: Sweep distance from 1,000 km to 50,000 km and plot Received Power 
           (Pr) versus Distance.

6. MATLAB CODE:
   clc; clear; close all;
   f = 12e9; R = 38000e3; lam = 3e8/f;
   Gt = 10*log10(0.65*(pi*2.4/lam)^2);
   Gr = 10*log10(0.60*(pi*1.8/lam)^2);

   EIRP = 10*log10(100) + Gt - 2;
   FSPL = 20*log10(4*pi*R/lam);
   Pr = EIRP - FSPL + Gr - 1.5 - 2;
   N = 10*log10(1.38e-23*500*36e6);
   CN = Pr - N;
   M = CN - 10;

   fprintf('EIRP = %.2f dBW\nFSPL = %.2f dB\nPr = %.2f dBW\nN = %.2f dBW\nC/N = %.2f dB\nMargin = %.2f dB\n', EIRP, FSPL, Pr, N, CN, M);

   d = linspace(1e3, 5e4, 20)*1e3;
   plot(d/1e3, EIRP - 20*log10(4*pi*d/lam) + Gr - 3.5, 'o-', 'LineWidth', 1.5);
   xlabel('Distance (km)'); ylabel('Received Power Pr (dBW)'); title('Received Power vs Distance'); grid on;

7. EXPECTED OUTPUT & GRAPH ANALYSIS:
   - Command Window Numerical Output:
     Gt = 47.72 dBi | Gr = 44.87 dBi | EIRP = 65.72 dBW | FSPL = 205.62 dB
     Pr = -98.53 dBW | N = -126.05 dBW | C/N = 27.52 dB | Margin = +17.52 dB
   - Output Plot (Pr vs Distance): Logarithmic decay curve showing that as 
     distance increases from 1,000 km to 50,000 km, received power drops 
     smoothly from -67 dBW down to -101 dBW.

8. CONCLUSION:
   The Satellite Link Budget was evaluated in MATLAB. Despite a massive Free 
   Space Path Loss of 205.62 dB over 38,000 km, high-gain dish antennas yield 
   an achieved C/N of 27.52 dB and a positive Link Margin of +17.52 dB, 
   confirming that the satellite link is highly feasible.

EXPERIMENT 8: SATELLITE ORBIT & GROUND TRACK SIMULATION
================================================================================

1. AIM:
   To simulate circular satellite orbits—Low Earth Orbit (LEO), Medium Earth 
   Orbit (MEO), and Geostationary Earth Orbit (GEO)—in 3D and plot their 
   orbital periods and sub-satellite ground tracks in MATLAB.

2. APPARATUS / SOFTWARE REQUIRED:
   - PC / Laptop with MATLAB installed

3. BLOCK DIAGRAM (ORBITAL MECHANICS & GROUND TRACK PROJECTION):
   +-------------------+      +-------------------+      +-------------------+
   |  Orbit Selection  | ---> |  Kepler's 3rd Law | ---> |  3D Orbit Plot    |
   | (LEO / MEO / GEO) |      | T = 2*pi*sqrt(r^3 |      | (Earth Sphere +   |
   +-------------------+      |          / mu)    |      |  Circular Path)   |
                              +-------------------+      +-------------------+
                                       |
                                       v
                              +-------------------+
                              | 2D Ground Track   |
                              | (Lat vs Lon Map)  |
                              +-------------------+

4. THEORY & FORMULAS:
   According to Kepler's Third Law, the orbital period (T) of a satellite 
   depends on the cube of its orbital radius (r = Re + h) from Earth's center:

   - Orbital Radius:
     r = Re + h   (where Earth radius Re = 6371 km)

   - Kepler's Third Law (Orbital Period):
     T = 2 * pi * sqrt(r^3 / mu)   (where mu = 398,600.4418 km^3/s^2)

   - Standard Orbit Classes:
     1) LEO (h = 500 km)    -> r = 6,871 km  -> T = 1.57 hours
     2) MEO (h = 20,200 km) -> r = 26,571 km -> T = 11.97 hours (GPS)
     3) GEO (h = 35,786 km) -> r = 42,157 km -> T = 23.93 hours (Stationary)

   A Ground Track is the path traced by the sub-satellite point on Earth's 
   surface directly beneath the orbiting satellite.

5. PROCEDURE:
   Step 1: Define altitudes [500, 20200, 35786] km and prompt user input (1-3).
   Step 2: Calculate total orbital radius r = 6371 + alt(c) and orbital period 
           T using Kepler's equation, printing T in hours.
   Step 3: Generate a 3D Earth sphere using sphere(30) and plot the 3D circular 
           equatorial orbit around Earth using plot3.
   Step 4: Map orbital angles to Longitude (-180 to +180 deg) and Latitude 
           (0 deg) to plot the equatorial ground track.

6. MATLAB CODE:
   clc; clear; close all;
   alt = [500 20200 35786];
   c = input('Enter 1 for LEO, 2 for MEO, 3 for GEO: ');
   r = 6371 + alt(c);
   T = 2*pi*sqrt(r^3/398600.4418);
   fprintf('Orbital Period = %.2f hours\n', T/3600);

   th = linspace(0, 2*pi, 500);
   [a,b,d] = sphere(30);
   figure;
   surf(6371*a, 6371*b, 6371*d, 'EdgeColor', 'none'); hold on; axis equal; grid on;
   plot3(r*cos(th), r*sin(th), 0*th, 'r--', 'LineWidth', 1.5);
   xlabel('X (km)'); ylabel('Y (km)'); zlabel('Z (km)'); title('3D Satellite Orbit Around Earth');

   figure;
   plot(mod(th*180/pi + 180, 360) - 180, 0*th, 'b', 'LineWidth', 2); grid on;
   xlim([-180 180]); ylim([-90 90]);
   xlabel('Longitude (deg)'); ylabel('Latitude (deg)'); title('Equatorial Ground Track');

7. EXPECTED OUTPUT & GRAPH ANALYSIS:
   - Command Window Output: Prints Orbital Period = 1.57 hours (for LEO), 
     11.97 hours (for MEO), or 23.93 hours (for GEO).
   - Figure 1 (3D Orbit): Renders the 3D Earth sphere surrounded by a red 
     dashed circular orbit in the equatorial plane.
   - Figure 2 (Ground Track): Displays a horizontal line along Latitude = 0 deg 
     spanning Longitude from -180 to +180 degrees.

8. CONCLUSION:
   Satellite orbits and ground tracks were simulated in MATLAB. Higher altitude 
   increases orbital radius and orbital period according to Kepler's Third Law, 
   with GEO satellites achieving a 23.93-hour sidereal period to appear 
   stationary above Earth's equator.

EXPERIMENT 9: SYSTEM NOISE, C/N, AND G/T ANALYSIS
================================================================================

1. AIM:
   To simulate receiver Thermal Noise Power (N), Carrier-to-Noise Ratio (C/N), 
   and Earth Station Figure of Merit (G/T) in MATLAB and analyze their variation 
   with System Noise Temperature (T) and Antenna Gain (G).

2. APPARATUS / SOFTWARE REQUIRED:
   - PC / Laptop with MATLAB installed

3. BLOCK DIAGRAM (SATELLITE RECEIVER NOISE & G/T CHAIN):
   +-------------------+      +-------------------+      +-------------------+
   | Receiving Dish    | ---> | Low Noise Amp     | ---> |  IF Filter &      |
   | Antenna (Gain G)  |      | (LNA, Low T_LNA)  |      |  Receiver (BW B)  |
   +-------------------+      +-------------------+      +-------------------+
                                                                   |
                                                                   v
                                                         +-------------------+
                                                         | Output Quality:   |
                                                         | N = kTB, C/N, G/T |
                                                         +-------------------+

4. THEORY & FORMULAS:
   Random thermal motion of electrons inside antennas and receiver circuits 
   generates Additive White Gaussian thermal noise:

   - Thermal Noise Power:
     N (Watts) = k * T_sys * B
     N (dBW)   = 10 * log10(k * T_sys * B)

   - Carrier-to-Noise Ratio (C/N):
     C/N (dB) = Pc (dBW) - N (dBW)

   - Earth Station Figure of Merit (G/T):
     G/T (dB/K) = G (dBi) - 10 * log10(T_sys)

   Where:
     k     = Boltzmann's Constant = 1.38 x 10^-23 J/K (-228.6 dBW/K/Hz)
     T_sys = Equivalent System Noise Temperature (Kelvin)
     B     = Receiver Noise Bandwidth (Hz)
     G     = Receiving Antenna Gain (dBi)

   A higher G/T ratio indicates a more sensitive receiving station capable of 
   detecting weaker satellite signals.

5. PROCEDURE:
   Step 1: Set nominal values: T = 150 K, G = 35 dBi, Pc = -100 dBW, 
           B = 1 MHz, and k = 1.38e-23 J/K.
   Step 2: Calculate and print nominal Noise Power N (-146.84 dBW), C/N ratio 
           (46.84 dB), and Figure of Merit G/T (13.24 dB/K).
   Step 3: Vary T from 50 K to 500 K and G from 20 dBi to 50 dBi.
   Step 4: Plot four subplots: N vs T, C/N vs T, G/T vs G, and G/T vs T.

6. MATLAB CODE:
   clc; clear; close all;
   T = 150; G = 35; Pc = -100; k = 1.38e-23; B = 1e6;
   N = @(T) 10*log10(k*T*B);
   fprintf('Noise Power = %.2f dBW | C/N = %.2f dB | G/T = %.2f dB/K\n', N(T), Pc-N(T), G-10*log10(T));

   Tr = 50:10:500;
   subplot(2,2,1); plot(Tr, N(Tr)); xlabel('Temp T (K)'); ylabel('Noise N (dBW)'); title('Noise Power vs Temp'); grid on;
   subplot(2,2,2); plot(Tr, Pc - N(Tr)); xlabel('Temp T (K)'); ylabel('C/N (dB)'); title('C/N vs Temp'); grid on;
   subplot(2,2,3); plot(20:50, (20:50) - 10*log10(T)); xlabel('Gain G (dBi)'); ylabel('G/T (dB/K)'); title('G/T vs Antenna Gain'); grid on;
   subplot(2,2,4); plot(Tr, G - 10*log10(Tr)); xlabel('Temp T (K)'); ylabel('G/T (dB/K)'); title('G/T vs Temp'); grid on;

7. EXPECTED OUTPUT & GRAPH ANALYSIS:
   - Command Window Output:
     Noise Power = -146.84 dBW | C/N = 46.84 dB | G/T = 13.24 dB/K
   - Subplot 1 (Noise vs Temp): Logarithmic increase in noise floor from 
     -151.6 dBW at 50 K to -141.6 dBW at 500 K.
   - Subplot 2 (C/N vs Temp): Logarithmic decrease in C/N from 51.6 dB down to 
     41.6 dB as system temperature increases.
   - Subplot 3 (G/T vs Antenna Gain): Straight linear line with a 1:1 slope 
     (1 dB increase in G yields 1 dB/K increase in G/T).
   - Subplot 4 (G/T vs Temp): Decaying logarithmic curve showing G/T dropping 
     from 18.01 dB/K to 8.01 dB/K as noise temperature rises.

8. CONCLUSION:
   System noise, C/N, and G/T were analyzed in MATLAB. Higher system noise 
   temperature increases thermal noise floor and degrades both C/N and G/T, 
   demonstrating why low-noise front-end amplifiers (LNAs) and high-gain 
   antennas are critical in satellite ground stations.
